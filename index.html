Australian chicken, from plate to paddock

The Chicken Nation

Fifty years ago, beef ruled the Australian dinner table. Today the average Australian eats more chicken than pork and beef combined. This is the story of how that happened, what it costs, what it is worth and where all those birds are raised.

01

The rise of chicken on Australian plates

Start with what is on the plate today. In 2024–25 the average Australian ate almost 54 kg of chicken, twice as much as pork and close to three times as much as beef.

It was not always like this. In the mid-1970s the average Australian ate more than 60 kg of beef a year and only about 12 kg of chicken. Over the next five decades the two lines crossed. Chicken was always ahead of pork and lamb; beef was its only real rival.

Why the switch? Price is a big part of the answer. Since 1980–81 the price of chicken has roughly doubled, while beef, lamb and everyday prices in general have risen about five-fold. Compared with other goods, chicken has become one of the cheapest ways to put meat on the table.

The Consumer Price Index (CPI) tracks how the price of the same item changes over time. Each line is set to 100 in 1980–81, the first year with data for all four meats, so the end value shows how many times more each item costs today. The dashed line is the average of all prices, a measure of general inflation. Source: ABS Consumer Price Index, as published in ABARES, Agricultural Commodity Statistics 2025, Table 12.3.

02

Chicken's place in the livestock economy

Eating more chicken means selling more chickens. In 2024–25 Australian farms sold poultry for meat worth $4.1 billion. That is fourth among the six main livestock sectors, behind cattle, milk and sheep, but chicken is the sector that has grown fastest.

Fastest growth of the six sectors

Poultry sales rose 42% in four years.

From $2.88 billion in 2020–21 to $4.10 billion in 2024–25. Cattle rose 39%, pigs 30%, sheep 29% and milk 27%, while wool fell 10%. These are dollar values, so part of every increase is higher prices rather than more animals.

Read the bars from left to right. Each bar starts where the one before it ended, and the last bar is the total rise of $8.6 billion, from $27.8 billion to $36.4 billion. Poultry added $1.22 billion, almost as much as milk, and about one seventh of all the growth.

The dotted lines connect each bar to the next. Wool, the only sector that fell, steps down. Cattle dominates because it is by far the biggest sector. Source: ABS, Australian Agriculture: Livestock, 2024–25, Tables 1 and 2.

Of the $36.4 billion earned from livestock in 2024–25, poultry meat made up 11.3%. Animals sold for meat brought in more than three quarters of the total; products taken from living animals, milk and wool, made up the rest.

"Poultry (meat)" counts birds sold for meat only. Eggs are not included in these 2024–25 tables. Local value is the value of production at the farm gate, after marketing costs such as freight are taken out. Source: ABS, Australian Agriculture: Livestock, 2024–25, Tables 1 and 2.

03

Mapping Australia's chicken production

Most of the country raises no chickens at all. Meat chickens are grown in sheds close to the big cities, near the processing plants and the people who buy the meat. Egg farms are spread further inland.

04

The production story over time

Chicken output has grown almost every year for six decades. Since 1967 the number of chickens processed has fallen in only nine years, and never by more than about 7% nationally.

A rare setback

2017–18 is the only recent year when Victoria and Queensland both fell by more than 10%.

NSW grew 6.5% that year, so the national total fell by just 2.6%. The data show where production moved, not why it moved.

05

Beyond state borders: regional hotspots

The map in Chart 7 showed where production is densest. Ranking regions by their total value tells a different story: the biggest producers are large farming regions inland, not the city fringe.

The Riverina in southern NSW alone produced $305 million of chicken meat, almost one in every seven dollars among regions with published figures. With Darling Downs–Maranoa and Geelong, the top three regions supply 29%.

Hollow dots: the ABS rates the estimate as less reliable. Shares are of the $2.06 billion total for the 56 regions with published figures; the 14 regions in SA and WA are confidential and not included. Source: ABS, Value of Agricultural Commodities Produced, 2020–21.

Three things to take away

Price changed the plate

Chicken passed beef in the early 2000s while its price rose far less than other meats and than prices in general.

A fast-growing sector

Poultry meat is a ninth of livestock value, but it grew 42% in four years, faster than any other sector.

Concentrated in a few places

Three states earn 72% of the value. Production is densest on the city fringe, but the Riverina is the single biggest region.

Definitions and method

Local value
Value of production at the farm gate, after marketing costs. Nominal dollars, not adjusted for inflation.
np (not published)
The ABS hides figures that could reveal a single business. These are shown in grey, never estimated.
Price index
Each CPI series ÷ its 1980–81 value × 100.
Density
Region's production value ÷ its land area in km².
Yearly change
(this year ÷ last year − 1) × 100.
Financial year
1 July to 30 June; quarterly figures are added up only when all four quarters exist.
